Claim-by-claim investigation

The airplane hum in your recording can pinpoint where it was made

A YouTube video claims that the faint, often inaudible drone of a passing propeller plane — buried in the background of an ordinary recording — encodes enough information to locate that recording to within about 100 metres, anywhere in the continental US, using public flight-tracking archives. This page checks every claim in the video against the published literature and primary sources, and synthesises what the method is, whether it works, and what it means.

Video: “I discovered a sound that leaks your exact location (and it’s everywhere)” Creator: colsto (pseudonymous) Published: 2 Nov 2026 Reach: ~586K views within ~13 hours (channel mirror, 3 Nov 2026) Watch: youtube.com/watch?v=UytYrhYLOKI

Bottom line: The physics and every building block of the method are real, textbook, and in several cases decades old. The headline demonstration — an unknown recording located to ~100 m, continent-wide — rests, as of 3 Nov 2026, only on the creator’s own video. He released no code or data, and no independent replication or expert review had appeared within the first day. The claims are mostly supported at the level of principle, partially supported where the video generalises, and the ~100 m result itself is unverifiable from outside so far.

What this is, and how it works

A propeller aircraft radiates a strong, steady tone at its blade-pass frequency — typically around 100 Hz — plus a ladder of harmonics. As the plane approaches, passes overhead, and recedes, the Doppler effect bends that tone: high on approach, dropping through the true frequency at the closest point of approach (CPA), low on departure. Plotted on a spectrogram, each flyover draws a distinctive S-shaped curve. That curve is a fingerprint of the plane’s speed and its closest distance to the microphone — and every commercial and most general-aviation flights broadcast their position, altitude, and velocity about once a second via ADS-B, in archives anyone can obtain. The method inverts the usual problem: instead of asking “where is that plane?”, it asks “given this curve, where must the microphone have been?”

The pipeline, as documented in the video’s own description:

1

Extract the curves

From the recording’s spectrogram, pull the Doppler curve of each propeller flyover — the fundamental near 100–130 Hz or any clean harmonic.

2

Measure speed & distance

The total frequency drop gives the aircraft’s speed; how sharply the curve transitions at CPA gives the slant distance from plane to microphone.

3

Pull the flight archive

Historical ADS-B for the recording’s date, filtered in Python by ICAO type code to keep propeller aircraft and drop jets.

4

Filter candidates

On a 200 m candidate grid, keep only points consistent with the measured curves — in the main demo, two flyovers separated by exactly 920 seconds, plus a “silence” constraint where no flyover should be heard.

5

Forward-model the survivors

For each surviving candidate, simulate the exact curve: CPA and sound-propagation delay from 3D slant distance using a digital elevation model, and radial velocity at the time the sound was emitted, interpolated from ADS-B.

6

Score and rank

A locked two-event scoring method ranks candidate locations. A repeat run on a single 1-minute clip of known time, searched US-wide, reportedly ranked the true location 1st.

One honesty note from the creator himself: “No code or tools are being released.” Everything about the final result therefore has to be taken on the strength of the video and its unusually detailed description.

Try the physics yourself

This is the core of Claim 2 and Claim 3, as an interactive model. A plane flies a straight line at constant speed past a microphone. The amber curve is the physically correct prediction: frequency computed from the plane’s radial velocity at the moment each bit of sound was emitted (sound takes seconds to arrive, and the plane moves hundreds of metres in that time). The blue curve is the naive version, using the plane’s position when the sound is received. They are not the same curve shifted in time — the transition region is warped asymmetrically, which is exactly why the video insists on emission-time modelling. Claim by claim

1 · The propeller tone: ~100–130 Hz, often inaudible, goes through walls

Supported

Propeller noise is dominated by a fundamental tone at the blade-pass frequency (rotation rate × number of blades) plus diminishing harmonics. The literature puts that fundamental “typically around 100 Hz,” with the exact value varying by type and RPM. At cruise altitudes the Doppler-shifted blade-pass tone and its low harmonics are the principal component heard on the ground, because the atmosphere acts as a low-pass filter that favours exactly these low frequencies.

  • Inaudible but measurable is physiologically normal. The hearing threshold at 100 Hz is ~26.5 dB SPL (vs ~0 dB at 1–4 kHz); a 100 Hz tone needs roughly 20 dB more level than a 1 kHz tone to sound equally loud. A tone can sit well above a microphone’s noise floor while remaining below conscious perception, especially masked by indoor ambient sound.
  • It really is everywhere. In one acoustical-oceanography study, the first ~10 harmonics of a light aircraft were detectable in air, in the water column, and on sensors buried ~1 m in the seabed.
  • Building penetration is standard acoustics: low-frequency sound travels long distances and passes through ordinary walls and structural gaps that absorb higher frequencies.

InterNoise 2014, “Adapting a propeller noise model for aircraft at cruising altitudes” — acoustics.asn.au (PDF); Buckingham & Richardson, “A light aircraft as a low-frequency sound source,” SciELO; ISO 226 / equal-loudness data via AES.

2 · The curve’s shape encodes speed, distance — and manoeuvres

Supported

This is not a new discovery; it is an established, published estimation method. The standard formulation fits a four-parameter model — speed, source frequency, CPA time, CPA slant range — to the instantaneous-frequency curve read off a spectrogram.

  • Ferguson (1992) estimated a propeller aircraft’s altitude and speed from a single ground microphone by fitting a Doppler model to the blade-rate variation, validated against the aircraft’s own records at 250–1,500 ft and 150–250 kt.
  • Seppi, Tape & Fee (2025) give the explicit recipe: the difference between approaching and receding frequencies yields speed, and the slope of the curve at CPA yields d₀, the slant distance at closest approach.
  • A US patent on acoustic airport surveillance describes the same processing: Doppler CPA analysis “provides estimates of time at the closest point of approach, as well as slant range and speed.”
  • Deviations are signal, not noise. Received frequency is a direct function of radial velocity at emission, so any turn, acceleration, or RPM change necessarily reshapes the curve. Seppi et al. could even detect RPM differences between a climbing and a descending flightseeing aircraft.

Ferguson, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 92(3):1403–1407 (1992) — abstract; Seppi et al., The Seismic Record (2025) — article and supplement (PDF); US Patent 8,059,489 — Justia.

3 · You must model the sound at emission time, not reception time

Supported

Standard aeroacoustics. Sound received now was emitted earlier, from where the source was — the retarded-time relation tret = t − |x − y(tret)|/c has to be solved, not approximated away. Because slant range changes by kilometres across a flyover, propagation delay changes by seconds across the event; at 50–70 m/s the aircraft moves hundreds of metres during the delay. Seppi et al.’s forward model makes the consequence concrete: the observed curve is a warped, not merely translated, version of the emission-time curve — which is what the interactive model above shows. The video’s description confirms the creator did exactly this (“emission-time radial velocity from interpolated ADS-B data”), and his emphasis on it is justified.

Embry-Riddle, “Aeroacoustics of Flight Vehicles” — eaglepubs.erau.edu; Casalino (2003), J. Sound & Vibration 261 — acoustique.ec-lyon.fr (PDF).

4 · ADS-B archives: “essentially all flights,” purchasable, prop/jet by type code

Partially supported

What’s real: ADS-B broadcasts position, altitude, speed and identity roughly every second; ADS-B Exchange archives global snapshots every 5 s since March 2020, plus per-aircraft and 2 Hz “hires” traces, and historical data is a commercial product. Prop-vs-jet mapping is straightforward — ICAO Doc 8643 type designators carry an engine character (P = piston, T = turboprop/turboshaft, J = jet; e.g. C172 = L1P, A320 = L2J) — and the video says it used exactly that.

Where it overstates:

  • “Essentially all flights” is too strong. Under 14 CFR 91.225, ADS-B Out is required only in Class A, B and C airspace, the 30 NM Mode C veil, and Class E at/above 10,000 ft MSL. Below 10,000 ft outside that airspace it isn’t required, and aircraft without an engine-driven electrical system are exempt — and the small piston aircraft that make the loudest low flyovers are precisely the segment most likely to be unequipped outside rule airspace.
  • Coverage gaps. Crowdsourced receiver networks have line-of-sight, terrain and density limits; a low aircraft in rural or mountainous country can be clearly audible overhead and absent from trackers.
  • Purchase has friction today. ADS-B Exchange now sells only ongoing subscriptions with minimum annual commitments — “does not offer one-time data extracts or project-based access.” Free alternatives exist (see Claim 9).
  • One nuance cuts in the method’s favour: FAA LADD/PIA blocking scrubs the FAA feed and participating trackers, but per the FAA it “does not impact the ADS-B broadcast data” — unfiltered crowdsourced networks still capture those aircraft. It doesn’t help for aircraft with no ADS-B at all, or military traffic with transponders off.

ADS-B Exchange, Data Products and Sample Data; 14 CFR 91.225 scope — legalclarity.org; ICAO type descriptions — VATSIM Germany KB.

5 · The earlier thunder-sound geolocation, at ~5 km precision

Partially supported

Both predecessor videos exist: “Can you geolocate a video using background thunder sounds?” (418K views) and “I located a fan’s video using thunder sounds” (90K views). The first modelled the acoustic envelope of lightning strikes (logged from the Blitzortung / lightningmaps.org feed over ~3 weeks) against the recording’s band-passed RMS envelope by FFT cross-correlation, and was “validated blind against a known location.” The fan video’s description gives the concrete number: 2.2 km error, limited by the public Blitzortung dataset’s ~3 km strike deviation, with ~500 m judged feasible given strikes from multiple directions and better data.

The specific “~5 km” figure for the first video could not be verified — no transcript or description states a final error. It’s the right order of magnitude given the 2.2 km result and the ~3 km dataset floor, but as a precise number it is unverifiable from accessible sources.

Channel listing — clipzag.com; thunder videos — SnbkIm56TfI, 6a7Z0I3LAnE.

9 · Feasibility: what replication actually takes

Feasible, with caveats

The economics have quietly flipped in the method’s favour, because free ADS-B history now exists. adsb.lol publishes a full global day free (ODbL): daily tarballs of 1.6–1.8 GB, converting to ~45 parquet files of ~1M records each (~45M records/day). The “~50 GB of parquet for one day” figure is plausible for the fuller commercial feed (ADS-B Exchange’s stream runs to ~1.2 billion records/day) but it is not a floor — a usable global day is ~2 GB compressed from the free source. The forward model itself is analytic and cheap (Seppi et al. fitted 1,193 crossings with a 5-parameter model), and the creator’s filter-then-score recipe — velocity/CPA pre-filter, then the inter-flyover time offset, then forward-scoring survivors on a 200 m grid — reduces a continental search to a tractable Python pipeline. No exotic hardware is implied by any source.

Required inputs: the recording’s date; at least one — in the main demo two, 920 s apart — sufficiently close prop flyovers during the clip; audio that retains the ~100 Hz region. The second demo additionally had known time of day and needed only 1 minute / one flyover.

adsb.lol data scale and hobbyist DuckDB/parquet analysis — tech.marksblogg.com; feed comparison — ClickHouse adsb.exposed.

Prior art: new trick, or old science?

The components are 30+ years old in the literature. What appears genuinely new is the inversion — locating an unknown consumer recording by matching its Doppler curves against archived flight tracks over a continent. No published prior demonstration of that exact inversion was found.

  • 1992 — single-microphone Doppler estimation. Ferguson estimates a prop aircraft’s speed and altitude from one ground microphone (Claim 2). Closed-form solutions followed (Timlelt et al., 2017), as did microphone-array trajectory estimation (Tong et al., JASA 2016).
  • 2002–2004 — the Doppler comb as a measuring instrument. Buckingham et al. used propeller Doppler combs to measure sediment sound speed in the ocean.
  • 2013 — the forensic cousin. Electric Network Frequency (ENF) analysis timestamps and authenticates recordings from mains hum and is used by the Metropolitan Police Forensic Audio Lab; Garg, Hajj-Ahmad & Wu (ICASSP 2013) explicitly proposed geo-location estimation from ENF signals. “Inaudible background signature leaks time/place” is an established forensic research programme.
  • 2020 — institutional money. The US National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency’s Soundscapes Competition paid ~$100K for algorithms to geolocate recordings from ambient sound (city-level classification).
  • 2025 — the closest analogue. Seppi, Tape & Fee analysed 303 seismometers and 1,216 known flights across 48 types, estimating flight parameters from Doppler curves and checking them against Flightradar24 ground truth. The difference: their sensors’ locations were known and the goal was identifying the aircraft; colsto inverts it to find the recorder. Published about a year before the video.

Reaction so far: within the first day, Hacker News carried the video twice (32 points and 9 points); the visible comments were enthusiastic and uncritical (“the coolest thing I’ve ever seen on YouTube”; “I would assume the three letter agencies have been using this forever”). It was also posted to Lobsters. No tech-press article, expert critique, or sceptical analysis was found in news, Reddit, or X-indexed sources within ~24 hours — flag that as time-limited: absence on day one is not absence of later scrutiny.

Seppi et al. press release — EurekAlert; ENF geolocation — Garg et al., ICASSP 2013 (PDF); NGA Soundscapes — nga.mil; HN reaction — Algolia search.

Do the defenses work?

(a) “Just high-pass filter it” — Unreliable as a casual fix

The prop fundamental (~100–130 Hz) sits inside the voice pitch range — adult male speech runs 85–180 Hz, female 165–255 Hz — so any high-pass gentle enough to keep voice sounding natural (cutoff ≲100 Hz, the typical 6–12 dB/octave “rumble filter”) leaves the fundamental largely intact. And the harmonics are the loophole in the other direction: blade-pass harmonics at 2×, 3×, 4× (200–520+ Hz) are Doppler-shifted copies of the same curve, and the published methods work from any clean harmonic. Even a telephone-style 300 Hz high-pass — which would kill the fundamental and damage voice naturalness — leaves the 3rd and higher harmonics carrying the full Doppler shape. Removing all of them means filtering away most of the speech band itself.

Where the video overreaches: harmonics diminish in amplitude and suffer more atmospheric absorption over distance, so a steep, deliberately chosen filter chain would in practice defeat distant, faint flyovers. “Not reliable as a casual fix” is supported; “cannot be filtered” would not be.

(b) “Talk over it” — Doesn’t block extraction

Extraction happens in the frequency domain: a persistent narrowband tone occupies specific spectrogram bins for tens of seconds while speech is broadband and time-varying, and log-scale spectrograms compress dynamic range so weak narrowband components stay visible alongside loud ones. It’s the same reason ENF analysts routinely pull a faint 50/60 Hz hum out from under speech. The limits the video doesn’t stress: clipping/saturation, aggressive automatic gain control, or a foreground that is itself a loud sustained tone in the same bins would break it.

(c) Phone calls: codecs and noise suppression — Plausible for codecs; unverified for suppression

This is the video’s softest claim. For codecs alone it’s plausible: the EVS codec’s only built-in high-pass is at ~20 Hz and it supports fullband audio (20 Hz–20 kHz) designed for generic audio as well as speech; Opus fullband likewise codes from near-DC to 20 kHz. Neither inherently strips a 100–130 Hz tone. Noise suppression cuts the other way, though: classical spectral subtraction and RNNoise-style suppressors are specifically designed to remove steady non-speech tones — tool documentation says steady noise (fan, AC, mains hum) “goes almost completely.” A Doppler tone is, however, non-stationary — it sweeps ±15–20% in frequency across the pass — which stationary-noise estimators track poorly, and suppression is app- and device-dependent, often absent or mild. No published experiment was found that runs a real flyover recording through Opus/EVS plus production noise suppression and measures curve survival. The creator’s in-video test is currently the only evidence: treat as unverified.

EVS codec white paper (Nokia) — nokia.com; Opus settings (Xiph) — wiki.xiph.org; RNNoise behaviour — stayput.dev.

Limits & failure modes

The method is real but brittle: it needs the sky, the archive, and the recording chain to all cooperate. The honest failure list:

1 · The plane isn’t in the data — the biggest hole

Non-equipped GA outside rule airspace, transponder-off traffic, and low-altitude/rural receiver gaps silently remove candidate flyovers. The plane you hear may simply not exist in the archive.

2 · The atmosphere doesn’t read the textbook

Effective sound speed varies with temperature and wind profile (Seppi et al. used a modelled 320 m/s, not 343); refraction creates acoustic shadow zones where a geometrically close flyover is inaudible, and turbulence smears the spectral lines the method reads.

3 · The recording chain

Phone hardware is mostly innocent — a peer-reviewed study found tested phones emphasise 85–140 Hz and roll off only below ~30 Hz. The real risks are app-level: voice-recorder high-pass and noise suppression, AGC, wind noise (broadband, concentrated at low frequencies, masking exactly the band of interest), and platform re-encoding. YouTube’s Opus (~128 kbps, fullband) should preserve the tone reasonably; aggressive short-form pipelines are untested.

4 · Ambiguity with weak inputs

One flyover constrains the recorder only to a locus of positions consistent with a single (speed, CPA-distance, CPA-time) triple. The headline precision came from two events with a fixed 920 s offset plus a silence constraint — or, in the second demo, from known time of day. One faint flyover with unknown time is a much weaker problem.

5 · RPM instability

Prop RPM — hence the fundamental — shifts with climb, descent and power settings. That both adds signal (Claim 2) and breaks naive constant-frequency curve extraction.

Smartphone microphone response — Awan et al., J. Voice, PMC; refraction/shadow zones — Wu & Redonnet (PDF).

Implications & applications

Privacy: audio is location data

The deepest implication is the one in the title: a recording’s background can carry its location even when nothing in the foreground does, and even when the carrier sound is inaudible to the person who made the recording. Anyone who publishes audio or video recorded outdoors — or near a window — in airspace with ADS-B coverage may be publishing a latent location fix, recoverable after the fact from public archives, at hobbyist cost. Stripping metadata does not touch this; the leak is in the waveform itself. The casual defenses people reach for (a rumble filter, talking over it) don’t reliably close it, per Claim 8.

Forensics and verification

The same property is a tool. The technique slots into an existing forensic family alongside ENF analysis: background signatures that timestamp and place recordings. Plausible uses include verifying or challenging the claimed origin of leaked or contested audio, corroborating whistleblower and conflict-zone recordings, and checking alibis — the creator’s own earlier work (locating an animal-abuse video, with follow-through to animal-welfare authorities) shows the OSINT pattern. Intelligence interest in exactly this capability is documented: NGA paid prize money for ambient-audio geolocation in 2020.

Science and sensing

Run in the forward direction — sensors of known location — the same curves are already doing science: Seppi et al. classified aircraft types and flight behaviour from 303 seismometers, and the Doppler comb has been used as an oceanographic sound source since 2002. Every phone recording is, in principle, a node in a passive acoustic sensor network nobody deployed on purpose.

What would settle it

The gap between “the physics is proven” and “the ~100 m continent-wide demo is proven” is one replication wide. The creator withholds his code, but nothing about the method is secret: the recipe is in his description, the data is free from adsb.lol, and the fitting literature is published. An independent replication — or a failed one — is the single most informative next event, and the thunder method’s history (independently re-implemented as open source) suggests this creator’s methods do get picked up and tested.

Open questions — what could not be verified

  • The ~100 m final accuracy and the “true location ranked 1st, US-wide” result — asserted only in the video/description; no code, data, or independent replication exists yet.
  • The exact “~5 km” figure for the first thunder video.
  • Codec + noise-suppression survival as a general claim — no independent experimental source found.
  • The creator’s real identity and credentials, and independent confirmation of the Primitive Technology and One Piece pinpoints.
  • Any expert scepticism of the new video — none found within ~24 h of publication (HN comments were enthusiastic); worth re-checking as scrutiny develops.
  • Whether the main demo’s reference recording was the creator’s own staged recording or a third party’s — the ground-truth procedure could not be independently confirmed from text sources.
